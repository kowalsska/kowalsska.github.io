SurfStatsIO - a prediction engine for pro surfing

Every year I play in my local WSL Fantasy League, and every year I picked my team the same way everyone else does: gut feel, a vague sense of who’s in form, and loyalty to the surfers I like watching. It’s a fun way to lose.

Competitive surfing is unusually hard to predict. Unlike most sports, the playing field changes between heats. The same athlete who looks unbeatable at a heavy reef break can be ordinary at a small beach break, and a wind shift halfway through an event can flip who’s favoured. All of that is knowable in advance, it’s just scattered across a decade of contest results and a forecast nobody is cross-referencing.

SurfStatsIO is my attempt to do that cross-referencing properly. It’s a prediction engine that takes years of World Surf League contest history and joins it against Surfline’s record of the ocean conditions on each of those days: the swell, the wind, the wave quality that every one of those heats was actually surfed in. Once history and conditions sit side by side, you can ask the question that matters: how does this particular surfer perform in these particular conditions? Out of that come probabilities for who wins this heat, who makes the podium, who wins the event, and how many fantasy points each athlete is likely to score.

The goal

The goal is narrow and slightly ridiculous, and I like it that way: win my local WSL Fantasy League and Bracket Challenge. Not build a product, not sell predictions to anyone, not write a paper. Just beat my friends.

That constraint turned out to be a good one. It means the output isn’t a dashboard of interesting numbers, it’s a decision: here are your picks for this event, by tier, for both the men’s and women’s tours, plus a per-heat winner call for the bracket. Everything else in the system exists to serve that one answer.

What it does

Point it at an upcoming event and it:

  • Pulls in the full historical record for the tour: two decades of events, every round, every heat, every individual wave score
  • Works out how good each athlete is right now rather than on career average, and how confident it should be in that judgement given how much they’ve actually competed
  • Correlates each athlete’s past results against the conditions they were surfed in, using Surfline data for every historical contest day: who actually performs in big powerful swell, who needs a light offshore, who falls away when it’s small and weak
  • Accounts for the venue on top of that: some surfers have a genuine track record at certain types of wave, and riding forehand versus backhand is a real edge
  • Pulls the Surfline forecast for the upcoming contest window and scores the field against the conditions they’re likely to get
  • Simulates the event bracket end to end, respecting who actually meets whom, and reports the outcomes as calibrated probabilities

Then it checks its own homework. Every prediction is saved as a snapshot before the event runs, and once results are in it scores itself against what actually happened. Being able to say “the model was confidently wrong about Bells last year” is more useful than any single set of picks.

Technical details

It’s a statistical forecasting pipeline rather than a ranked list with opinions attached. Every athlete carries a skill rating that updates after each event and comes with its own uncertainty, so a veteran with hundreds of heats and a rookie with three are never treated as equally well understood. Recent form sits on top of that, damped enough that a single spectacular heat can’t manufacture a title contender overnight.

Scores are normalised before anything is learned from them, because a 15-point heat total means something different on a six-foot day at Teahupo’o than in waist-high slop. Surfline conditions are joined to every contest day, each athlete’s record is scored against them, and all of it feeds a machine learning model that outputs the probability of advancing from a heat. Those outputs are calibrated, so a stated 70% converts about 70% of the time. The men’s and women’s tours are modelled separately.

The model is only ever trained on data that existed at the time and is backtested against events it never saw, which matters more than any individual signal in it. The per-heat probabilities then drive a simulation of the full elimination bracket, respecting the real draw, and that’s where the probability of winning the event, of making the podium, and the expected fantasy points all come from.

What I’ve left out is the part that matters: which signals earn their place, how they’re weighted, and the tuning that took this from plausible to useful.

What I learned

Mostly, I learned statistical modelling. I came to this as a software engineer, not a statistician, and predicting sport turned out to be a proper education in it: rating systems and what they actually measure, how to build features that carry signal instead of noise, how to handle small samples, and how to validate honestly. The vocabulary was the easy part. The judgement took much longer: knowing which number to distrust, and why a result that looks too good is usually a bug rather than a breakthrough.

The other half of that was tuning. Getting a model to run is a weekend; getting it to be good is everything after. Choosing hyperparameters and understanding what each does to the fit, pruning features that look important but aren’t, tuning the men’s and women’s tours separately because a smaller sample needs a different hand, and re-running the backtest after every change to see whether the idea I was fond of survived contact with the data. Most didn’t. Learning to delete my own clever ideas on the evidence was the most useful habit the project gave me.
